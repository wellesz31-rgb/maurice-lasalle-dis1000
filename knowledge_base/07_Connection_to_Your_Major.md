# Document 7: Connection to Your Major

## How Maurice Should Use This Document

This document covers the **Connection to Your Major (CtM)** project — the individual research-and-network thread that runs alongside the Weeks 12–15 Synthesis content. Use it to answer students' logistical and process questions: what's due each week, what a consultation note should include, how the evidence trail fits together, what the Final Pitch involves.

**Do not** use this document to draft a student's central claim, pick their sources for them, or write their Connection Map. That's the actual intellectual work of the assignment. If a student asks "what should my connection be," redirect them to their own research and, if needed, to Mr. Turner. Grading questions and creative/argument feedback still go to Mr. Turner directly, per Maurice's standing scope.

---

## Purpose

The Connection to Your Major project asks students to investigate a meaningful relationship between music and their major, intended major, or professional field. They'll use research, conversations with HPU community members, and feedback from others to develop and communicate that connection.

The point isn't just a final paper. Students are practicing:
- building an academic and professional network
- locating and using credible research
- connecting ideas across fields
- developing an evidence-based argument
- responding thoughtfully to feedback
- communicating what they've learned to others

Work develops across **Weeks 12–15**, in parallel with the Synthesis unit's weekly content (Continuity Map, Propaganda vs. Outcome Map, Knock-Out Comparison — see the Synthesis document for those).

## Week 12 — Build the Research Trail

### Community Event Debrief
A short debrief connecting back to the Week 11 Music and Community event — what happened, what stood out, and (if relevant) any connection to the emerging CtM topic.

### Build a Working Bibliography
At least **five credible sources**, including at minimum:
- one book or eBook
- one scholarly journal article

Students meet with a research librarian to strengthen their search, find additional sources if needed, and review the bibliography. For each source they add a brief note on the main idea/argument/evidence they expect to use and how it might contribute to the project.

**Timing note:** The library visit / CtM intro often actually happens on the Friday of Week 11, since that class session is freed up by the extension on the Music and Community assignment.

### Research Skills & Library Strategies

**Contacting the library:** Bryan Nicholls (bnicholls@highpoint.edu, Smith Library) is a good first contact for research consultations — check the library website for his current phone number and online scheduling system. Mention you're a DIS 1000 student working on the Connection to Your Major project. If Bryan isn't available, any reference librarian can help via the "Ask a Librarian" service, live chat, or the reference desk during walk-in hours.

**If the library is fully booked:** use the live chat service or email a reference librarian and document the conversation (screenshot or saved email); visit the reference desk during open hours; plan ahead rather than waiting until the day before a deadline.

**If you can't find enough sources:** use library databases (Music Index, Academic Search Complete, JSTOR, ProQuest) rather than just Google; expand your search terms or broaden to the wider field/era if your specific topic is thin; try the Amazon → library catalog trick — search Amazon for relevant books, note the ISBN, then search the library catalog using that ISBN.

**Using Wikipedia as a finding tool, not a source:** read it for a general overview and to identify angles, then scroll to its References/External Links section and track down the original sources it's built on. Cite those original sources — never Wikipedia itself.

**Citing sources in Chicago format:** identify the source type, then use HPU's Chicago Style Citation Guide (https://guides.highpoint.edu/chicagomanual). AI can explain general citation structure, but verify against the official guide. If using a citation generator, never copy-paste the result — check it against the guide and correct errors; students are responsible for citation accuracy, not the tool. Common mistakes: capitalization, punctuation, author name format, hanging indents.

**Google vs. library resources:** Google is fine for initial exploration and finding official sites, but library databases and subscriptions surface credible, peer-reviewed sources Google won't.

## Week 13 — Meet Your Field, Map the Connections, Develop Your Argument

### Meet Someone in Your Major
Students visit or schedule an appointment with a faculty member in their major or intended field, and use the conversation to learn more about the field — the discipline itself, internships/research opportunities, grad school, careers, important open questions, or possible connections to music.

Afterward they record:
- who they met and when
- 2–3 useful ideas from the conversation
- one way the conversation influenced their project's direction

### Create a Connection Map
A mind map or other visual map connecting the research sources — a node per source, key concepts/findings/evidence from each, connections/agreements/tensions/patterns among sources, and possible links to DIS 1000 ideas.

### Identify Main Points
3–5 important claims or points that could organize the final project. For each: the claim stated clearly, specific supporting evidence, and any relevant DIS 1000 connection.

### Develop an Argument Plan
A structured plan (detailed outline, structured notes, or similar) showing: the working central claim, major supporting points, which sources back each point, where course concepts contribute to the interpretation, and why the connection matters within the field.

### Week 13 Evidence
- Faculty Consultation Note
- Connection Map
- 3–5 Main Claims or Points
- Structured Outline or Argument Plan
- Any additional sources found via the faculty or librarian consultation

## Writing Center Consultation (Complete Anytime During Weeks 12–14)

Students shouldn't wait until the project is finished — they bring whatever stage will be most useful (Connection Map, argument plan, outline, partial draft, developing artifact). Good things to ask about: clarity of the central claim, organization, connections among ideas, use/integration of research evidence, transitions, or communicating to an audience outside the major.

They record: when they met, what they brought, the most useful feedback received, and one change they plan to make.

## Week 14 — Review, Feedback, and Light Revision

Thanksgiving week is shortened — **no new CtM assignment**. Students instead review what they've done, checking:
- Is my central connection clear?
- Am I actually using my research, not just listing/summarizing sources?
- Do my sources connect with one another?
- Have I incorporated something from my faculty conversation?
- Can I identify a meaningful DIS 1000 connection?
- Is there an important gap in my research or reasoning?
- What's the most important revision to make next?

If they haven't done the Writing Center consultation yet, this is their last easy chance — **the syllabus treats Writing Center Consultation and Revision Plan as alternatives**, so a student who's already done the Writing Center consultation can submit that note instead of a separate Revision Plan, and vice versa.

### Week 14 Evidence
- **Either** a Writing Center Consultation Note **or** a Revision Plan (one strength, one gap/problem, next revision) — not necessarily both

## Week 15 — Final Integration

### Final Project
Should demonstrate: a clear, meaningful connection between music and the student's major/field; effective use of credible research; synthesis rather than a string of summaries; a relevant DIS 1000 connection; evidence that conversations and feedback shaped their thinking; and clear communication for the intended audience.

### Research and Network Trail
The CtM ePortfolio page should show the visible process behind the final project:
- Connection to Major Research Question and Initial Source Trail
- Community Event Debrief
- Faculty Consultation Note
- Librarian Consultation Note
- Working Bibliography
- Connection Map
- Main Claims / Argument Plan
- Writing Center Consultation Note or Revision Plan
- Final Bibliography
- Final Project
- **Process Reflection** — a short reflection on how the project evolved and what shaped it (consultations, feedback, revisions)

### Final Pitch (Virtual Poster Session / STAR Pitch)
A 90-second pitch introducing the CtM project, using the STAR structure as a guide:

- **Situation** — What field/issue are you exploring? Where does music enter?
- **Task** — What specific connection did you investigate, and why was it worth it?
- **Action** — What research and consultations did you use? What was the most important discovery?
- **Result** — What does the investigation suggest about your field? How did the project change your thinking? What's the one idea you want your audience to remember?

This is delivered at the **Virtual Poster Session**, in the final exam slot (12/9, 3:30–4:30 PM). Students get to practice the pitch, get feedback, revise, and check their ePortfolio before presenting.

## Common Student Questions

*(Draft only — flag anything that needs adjusting.)*

**"What counts as a credible source?"** — Point them toward the librarian consultation; that's what it's for. A general rule of thumb: peer-reviewed, published by a reputable outlet, or recommended by a librarian or faculty member — not a random blog or an AI-generated summary.

**"What if my faculty contact doesn't respond?"** — Suggest they try a second person in the department, or ask Mr. Turner for a suggestion — don't let one non-response stall the whole project.

**"Does the Connection Map need to be a specific tool or format?"** — Not specified as fixed; a hand-drawn map, a slide, or a mapping tool all work as long as it shows sources, key ideas, and the connections between them.

**"Can I do the Writing Center or librarian consultation virtually?"** — Defer to HPU's current Writing Center / library policy rather than guessing.
